A normal xenon flash puts out a broad spectrum that spills well past 700nm—you just never see it because there's a visible-pass coating on the tube or a UV/IR cut filter in the assembly. Pull that filter, add a 720nm pass filter on the front of the head, and you have a portable IR light source that's effectively invisible to anyone not looking for it. The exposure is short, the subject can't brace for it, and the resulting files look like nothing else.

The modifications are simple but unforgiving:

- Removing the tube's UV/IR cut window without cracking the envelope or shifting the electrode alignment.
- Recalculating guide numbers from scratch—the 720nm filter costs roughly three to four stops, and TTL metering will lie to you because the sensor that drives it can't see IR.
- Replacing the flash's front element with IR-pass optical material, since standard glass and plastic coatings attenuate near-IR more than the datasheets admit.

What changes at capture time is more interesting than the hardware. Skin goes translucent, veins surface, fabric dyes that read as solid black in visible light turn out to be near-transparent, and tattoos frequently disappear entirely. It's the look that made airport surveillance film feel alien—except now you're walking around with it on a hot shoe. Worth being deliberate about where you point the thing.
